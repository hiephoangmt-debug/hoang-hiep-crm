My Son Sanctuary is a valley of ancient Cham brick temples about 35–40 km from Hoi An (45 minutes to 1 hour by car) and roughly 50–70 km from central Da Nang depending on the route (1 hour 15 to 1 hour 30 minutes). The site opens at 6:00 every day. In 2026, My Son tickets cost about VND 150,000 for international visitors and VND 100,000 for Vietnamese visitors (reference prices). The ticket includes the electric shuttle from the gate to the temples, the site museum and the daily Cham dance shows. The best plan is to arrive between 6:00 and 8:00, before the heat, watch the 9:00 dance at the foot of Group G, and be back in Hoi An for lunch. Half a day is enough for most visitors.

This guide is written by locals. Note: since 1 July 2025 the former Quang Nam province, where My Son is, has been part of Da Nang city. The site is now in Thu Bon commune, Da Nang (formerly Duy Phu commune, Duy Xuyen district, Quang Nam), so maps and tickets may say “Da Nang”.

Why visit My Son? A short history

My Son was the main religious centre of the Champa kingdom. For nearly a thousand years, from about the 4th to the 13th century, Cham kings built temples here to the Hindu god Shiva. The earliest temples were wooden. They were later replaced with fired-brick towers, laid so tightly that you can hardly see any mortar. Researchers still debate how it was done. The valley is ringed by hills, with Cat's Tooth Mountain (Hon Quap, called Mahaparvata, “great mountain”, by the Cham) rising behind it.

UNESCO listed My Son as a World Heritage Site in 1999, the same year as Hoi An Ancient Town. According to UNESCO, there are around 70 structures in several groups. War did enormous damage, above all the bombing of 1969. Tower A1, once considered the finest, is now a pile of bricks, and you can still see bomb craters beside the paths.

Restoration has taken decades. Polish architect Kazimierz Kwiatkowski (“Kazik”) worked here from the early 1980s. An Italian-supported project later restored Group G. From 2017 to 2022, the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) restored Groups A, H and K, and a new ASI project on Groups E and F began in February 2025.

What to see: the Cham temples group by group

French archaeologists named the temple groups with letters. The usual route starts at Group K near the shuttle stop, continues to B, C and D in the centre, then branches out to the hillside groups. The map at the gate shows the paths, and areas under restoration are fenced off.

Group K – the gateway

This is the first structure you see after the shuttle, standing on its own by the path. ASI stabilised it in its 2017–2022 project. You don't need long here, but the early light on the brick is lovely.

Groups B, C and D – the heart of the sanctuary

Spend most of your time here. The main tower, C1, still has its shape, with wall pilasters and carved deities. Around it are a gate tower, a boat-roofed storehouse for ritual objects, and sandstone linga–yoni altars. The two long halls, D1 and D2, were once used to prepare ceremonies and now display sandstone statues and reliefs found on site.

Group A – Tower A1 and the scars of war

Group A lies across the stream. Tower A1, the largest in the group, was destroyed by bombing in 1969. ASI stabilised what remains of Group A in 2017–2022. This is where you see most clearly how much My Son has lost.

Group G – on the hill, with the dance stage

Group G sits on a small hill and was restored in a project with Italian support. The foot of Group G is the stage for the 9:00 and 14:45 Cham dance shows, which are well worth catching.

Groups E and F – under restoration

Group E has Tower E7, which is still fairly intact. A Vietnam–India restoration project has been running on Groups E and F since 2025, so expect scaffolding or fencing. If the area is open, watching the work is interesting in itself.

Group H – on the hillside

Group H sits on the hillside and was restored by ASI along with Groups A and K. The slope up to Tower H is where Cham folk singing takes place on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays.

Each show lasts about 15–20 minutes, with Paranung drums, the Saranai horn and Apsara dance. The “Echoes of My Son” programme is held at Groups B–C–D on the 15th day of each lunar month (full moon). A separate night show, “Legendary My Son Night”, combines lighting, music and dance, and its dates are announced in advance. Times can change on holidays, so check when you buy your ticket.

Best time of day and month to visit

  • Best hours: 6:00–8:30 is cool and quiet, often with morning mist on the hills. After 10:00 it gets hot and crowded. Late afternoon after 15:00 is pleasant too, with low light on the brick, but watch the closing time.
  • February–April: dry and mild, the easiest months for walking.
  • May–August: fierce sun, with midday often above 35°C and little shade. Go early.
  • September–December: rainy season, peaking in October–November. Paths get slippery, and the site may pause visits during severe floods. The moss and low clouds are very photogenic, though. Bring a raincoat and shoes with good grip.

Suggested half-day plan and a My Son + Hoi An combo

  1. 5:15–5:30: leave Hoi An (or 4:45 from Da Nang).
  2. 6:15: buy tickets and take the shuttle. See Group K, then B–C–D and the D1–D2 displays.
  3. 8:00: cross to Group A, then up to G and H. Visit E–F if it is open.
  4. 9:00: watch the Cham dance at the foot of Group G (or 9:45 at the Performance House).
  5. 10:00: head back to Hoi An by car or by boat on the Thu Bon River.
  6. Afternoon – evening: rest at midday, stroll the old town in the afternoon and float a lantern on the Hoai River at night. See our Hoi An travel guide.

Practical tips for visiting My Son

  • Heat: bring a wide-brimmed hat, sunscreen and sunglasses. An umbrella helps in summer.
  • Water: carry at least 1 litre per person, as there are few places to buy drinks among the temples.
  • Shoes: trainers or strapped sandals. The paths are dirt, with slopes, and get slippery in the rain.
  • Dress: this is a sacred site, so cover your shoulders and knees.
  • Respect the ruins: don't climb the towers, fallen walls or altars. Don't sit on statues, touch or pick at the bricks, or write on anything.
  • Stay on the paths: the area was once mined and bombed, so only walk on signed trails.
  • Mosquitoes: the valley is forested, so bring repellent, especially in the rainy season.
  • Get a guide: hire an on-site guide or join a guided tour. Without context, many groups are just “piles of bricks”.

FAQ

How much are My Son tickets?

For reference in 2026: VND 150,000 for international visitors, VND 100,000 for Vietnamese visitors and VND 30,000 for children aged 5–15. The ticket includes the shuttle, the museum and the Cham dance shows. A price rise has been studied, so check at the counter.

What time does My Son open?

From 6:00 daily, closing around 17:00–18:00 depending on the season. Ticket sales usually run until about 17:00.

What time is the Cham dance at My Son?

Since 16 March 2026: 9:00 and 14:45 at the foot of Group G, and 9:45, 10:30, 11:15, 14:00 and 16:00 at the Performance House. Each show lasts about 15–20 minutes.

How long does it take from Hoi An to My Son?

It is about 35–40 km, or 45 minutes to 1 hour by car. From central Da Nang, allow about 1 hour 15 to 1 hour 30 minutes.

Which province is My Son in now?

Since 1 July 2025, My Son has been in Thu Bon commune, Da Nang city, after Quang Nam merged into Da Nang. It used to be in Duy Xuyen district, Quang Nam province.
